# The 7 Powers, Three Horizons, and Compounding Flywheels

Summaries of four published frameworks for multi-decade business strategy,
in our own words. This skill applies them to turn a day-one business into an
enduring, compounding institution with structural moats.

## 1. The 7 Powers framework

Source: Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy* (2016).

Power is the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential
returns. To create true power, a strategy must satisfy two simultaneous criteria:
1. **Benefit**: The condition must materially augment cash flow (either by raising
   prices or reducing costs).
2. **Barrier**: The condition must prevent existing and potential competitors from
   arbitraging that benefit away.

Without a barrier, any benefit is quickly copied and commoditized. The seven
structural powers:

1. **Scale Economies**: The per-unit cost of delivering a product or service
   declines as volume increases.
   - *Benefit*: Lower cost structure than any smaller competitor.
   - *Barrier*: High fixed costs that a follower cannot amortize across a smaller
     customer base (e.g., Netflix content spend, Amazon regional fulfillment hubs).

2. **Network Effects**: The value of the product to each customer increases as
   new users join the network.
   - *Benefit*: Ability to charge premium rates or lock in demand.
   - *Barrier*: Two-sided or multi-sided chicken-and-egg problem; a follower's
     isolated product has near-zero standalone value (e.g., eBay, Visa, LinkedIn).

3. **Counter-Positioning**: A newcomer adopts a novel, superior business model
   that incumbents cannot copy without inflicting severe collateral damage on
   their existing, highly profitable cash-cow core business.
   - *Benefit*: Cheaper, simpler, or more convenient offering.
   - *Barrier*: Incumbent management cannot rationalize self-cannibalization or
     channel conflict (e.g., Netflix DVD delivery vs. Blockbuster late fees;
     Vanguard low-cost index funds vs. Wall Street active fund management).

4. **Switching Costs**: The customer experiences substantial loss, expense, or
   operational friction if they switch to an alternative supplier.
   - *Benefit*: Pricing power and sustained high customer retention.
   - *Barrier*: The total cost to switch (data migration, staff retraining, risk
     of downtime, contractual penalties) exceeds the marginal benefits of a
     rival's product (e.g., enterprise ERPs, specialized developer workflows).

5. **Branding**: The objective ability to charge a persistent price premium or
   earn higher sales velocity stemming from historical quality, trust, and an
   emotional identity halo built over years.
   - *Benefit*: Price inelasticity and organic customer acquisition.
   - *Barrier*: Time, cultural salience, and cumulative consistent experiences
     cannot be bought quickly, even with massive competitor ad budgets
     (e.g., Tiffany, Apple, Hermès).

6. **Cornered Resource**: Preferential, exclusive access to a scarce, highly
   valuable asset that independently produces outsized economics.
   - *Benefit*: Unique product quality or cost advantages unavailable to peers.
   - *Barrier*: Legal exclusivity, regulatory rights, or idiosyncratic talent
     monopolies (e.g., Qualcomm patents, exclusive prime real estate, proprietary
     mineral deposits).

7. **Process Power**: Embedded company organization, operational routines, and
   tacit institutional knowledge that yield superior quality or lower costs,
   incapable of being replicated by inspection alone.
   - *Benefit*: Superior speed, defect reduction, or efficiency.
   - *Barrier*: Hysteresis and complexity; the routines are so deeply woven into
     company culture that outside competitors cannot reproduce them merely by
     hiring a consultant or reading the manual (e.g., Toyota Production System,
     TSMC semiconductor fabrication).

### The Power Progression

Powers do not appear overnight; they emerge at specific stages:
- **Origination (Pre-launch to early product-market fit)**: Typically only
  Counter-Positioning or Cornered Resources are available.
- **Takeoff (Rapid scaling)**: Scale Economies, Network Effects, and Switching
  Costs can be constructed.
- **Stability (Mature market)**: Branding and Process Power mature and harden
  defenses.

Questions this lens asks:
- Which power protects you in Year 1? (Usually Counter-Positioning).
- Which structural power will you build by Year 5?
- What prevents a well-funded copycat from taking your customers once you prove
  the model?

## 2. McKinsey's Three Horizons of Growth

Source: Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley, and David White, *The Alchemy of Growth*
(2000).

Sustainable companies avoid two fatal traps:
1. *The Operator Trap*: Focusing 100% of effort on immediate cash flow (Horizon 1),
   becoming vulnerable to sudden technological obsolescence or market shifts.
2. *The Dreamer Trap*: Chasing grand visionary platforms (Horizon 3) without an
   immediate cash engine, running out of money before the vision is reached.

The Three Horizons model balances both:

- **Horizon 1 (Defend and extend core business: Months 0 to 18)**:
  - Focus: Immediate cash flow, unit economics, and customer acquisition.
  - Metrics: Operating profit, contribution margin, break-even velocity, churn.
  - Milestone: Self-sustaining cash generation from a proven beachhead.

- **Horizon 2 (Build emerging businesses: Years 2 to 4)**:
  - Focus: Adjacencies, vertical integration, and structural scaling.
  - Strategy: Moving upstream (owning supply) or downstream (owning distribution),
    introducing recurring subscriptions, expanding to secondary markets.
  - Milestone: Horizon 2 generates substantial new top-line revenue and widens
    the moat around Horizon 1.

- **Horizon 3 (Create viable options for the future: Years 5 to 10+)**:
  - Focus: Transformative bets, industry standard platforms, infrastructure.
  - Strategy: Licensing proprietary technology, white-label networks, open
    ecosystems that turn competitors into customers.
  - Milestone: The company becomes the foundational platform in its category.

Questions this lens asks:
- What is the beachhead that funds tomorrow's expansion?
- What are the logical Horizon 2 extensions once the core product dominates?
- What is the 10-year platform play that makes the company indispensable?

## 3. The Compounding Flywheel

Source: Jim Collins, *Good to Great* (2001) and *Turning the Flywheel* (2019).

Enduring greatness never comes from a single breakthrough, a silver bullet, or
a lucky marketing campaign. It comes from pushing a heavy, giant flywheel turn
by turn, compounding momentum over years until the machine carries its own weight.

A valid flywheel must satisfy three rules:
1. **Direct causality**: Step A must inevitably drive Step B. If the connection
   relies on hope or loose correlation, the flywheel stalls.
2. **Reinforcing feedback loop**: The final step must loop directly back into
   Step A, accelerating the next rotation with less external force required.
3. **Moat expansion**: Each completed turn must make it harder for competitors
   to follow, widening the distance between you and the rest of the pasture.

Questions this lens asks:
- What is the single catalyst that starts the wheel spinning?
- How does step 2 logically and inevitably trigger step 3?
- How does the end of the loop make the next turn faster, cheaper, and more
  defensible?

## 4. Antifragile Strategy and the 10-Year Premortem

Source: Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder* (2012).

Systems respond to disorder in one of three ways:
- *Fragile*: Damaged or broken by volatility, shocks, and unexpected crises.
- *Robust*: Resists shocks and stays unchanged.
- *Antifragile*: Becomes stronger, smarter, and more dominant as a direct result
  of stress, volatility, and industry turmoil.

To survive a 10-year horizon, a business must build antifragile safeguards:
1. **The Barbell Architecture**: Couple an ultra-safe, low-risk, cash-generating
   foundation (85-90% of resources) with high-upside, asymmetric experiments
   (10-15% of resources). Never risk the core on speculative survival bets.
2. **Via Negativa (Removal of Fragility)**: Eliminate single points of failure
   before adding new features. Remove reliance on single suppliers, single
   advertising channels, or single regulatory exemptions.
3. **The 10-Year Premortem**: Assume it is 10 years into the future and the
   business has gone bankrupt. What killed it?
   - Threat A: Technological substitution / automation / AI commoditization.
   - Threat B: Platform / gatekeeper risk (changes in app stores, search
     algorithms, wholesale suppliers, or landlords).
   - Threat C: Capital or macro contraction (recession, cost of capital spike,
     input inflation).
   Design concrete operational hedges into the multi-year plan from Day 1.

Questions this lens asks:
- What single external shock could destroy this business in year 5?
- How does industry chaos (e.g., an economic downturn or platform policy shift)
  make this business stronger while killing weaker rivals?
- What is the structural hedge built into the operations?
