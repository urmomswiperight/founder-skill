# The Purple Cow and Breakthrough Differentiation

Summaries of two published frameworks for extreme differentiation, in our own words.
This skill applies them to transform a business from one of a hundred brown cows
into an unmistakable purple cow.

## The Purple Cow doctrine

Source: Seth Godin, *Purple Cow* (2003).

**The brown cow trap.** Drive past a farm pasture with a hundred cows. For the
first five minutes, the cows are interesting. By the tenth minute, you do not
even look out the window. They are all brown, all ordinary, all invisible.
Ninety-nine percent of businesses enter the pasture as just another brown cow:
they copy the category leaders, match their features, shave five percent off
the price, and wonder why nobody cares.

**The Purple Cow.** If one cow in that field were purple, everyone in the car
would stop, gasp, take out their phones, and tell their friends. A Purple Cow is
*remarkable*--literally worth remarking on. It is not just "very good." In a
crowded market, "very good" is the enemy because "very good" is boring, safe,
and forgettable.

**Built into the product, not painted on.** You cannot turn a brown cow into a
purple cow with purple spray paint. True remarkability is engineered into the
substance of the product or service itself: the ingredients, the process, the
pricing model, the delivery speed, or an intentional, radical constraint.

**Target the sneezers.** Never launch a Purple Cow to the mass market first.
Find the "sneezers"--the small, obsessed group of buyers who care intensely
about this specific problem (the otaku) and whose social identity depends on
recommending remarkable things first.

Questions this lens asks:
- If a customer drove past 100 of your competitors, why would they brake for you?
- What is the one thing built into the product that makes people talk without being asked?
- Who are the first 100 obsessed buyers (sneezers) who will spread this?

## The Breakthrough Advertising doctrine

Source: Eugene M. Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966).

**You cannot create mass desire; you can only channel it.** The copywriter does
not create the customer's hunger, insecurity, pride, or ambition. Those forces
already exist in millions of people. Your job is to channel that existing force
onto your specific product.

**The Five Stages of Market Sophistication.** How crowded is your pasture, and
what have buyers already heard?

1. *Stage 1 (First in market)*: Simple, direct claim. No one has promised this
   before ("Now! Lose ugly fat").
2. *Stage 2 (Second in market)*: The claim is known; push it to the believable
   limit ("Lose up to 47 pounds in 4 weeks").
3. *Stage 3 (Crowded market / The Brown Cow Pasture)*: Buyers have heard all the
   claims and no longer believe bigger promises. You must introduce a **New
   Mechanism**--the specific process, ingredient, or operating method that shows
   *how* the promise is finally fulfilled. The emphasis shifts from *what* the
   product does to *how* it works ("Floats fat right out of your body", "Luckies:
   they're toasted").
4. *Stage 4 (Elaborated Mechanism)*: Competitors introduce their own mechanisms.
   You must upgrade, tighten, or eliminate old limitations from the mechanism
   ("First no-diet wonder drug", "Dual filter for double the pleasure").
5. *Stage 5 (Exhausted / Cynical Market)*: Prospects no longer believe any claims
   or mechanisms. Direct advertising fails. You must shift completely to
   **Radical Identification and Redefinition**--the product becomes a symbol of
   who the buyer is, or redefines the entire category standard ("Blow some my
   way", "Think Small").

**The Unique Mechanism.** When promises sound alike, the mechanism is your
weapon.
- *Name it*: Give the proprietary method or ingredient a clear, memorable handle.
- *Describe it*: Show the physical reality of how it operates, in vivid plain
  words that make the outcome feel inevitable.
- *Feature it*: Put the mechanism right at the front of your proposition.

**The Concentration Technique (Direct Contrast).** Never attack competitors
merely to slander them; that breeds skepticism. Instead, expose the hidden tax
or built-in compromise that all 100 brown cows force the customer to accept
(e.g., spark plug electrodes that corrode; standard coffee that gives jitters;
gyms that lock you into yearly contracts). Then lay out a strict side-by-side
contrast:
- *Bad (The 100 Brown Cows)*: What they make you tolerate.
- *Good (The Purple Cow)*: How your mechanism eliminates that flaw permanently.

**Redefinition (Concept-Judo).** Turn an apparent liability into an asset.
When Lifebuoy soap smelled medicinal, they did not hide the smell; they branded
it as proof of bacteria-killing power. When Volkswagen was tiny, they ran "Think
Small" and "Lemon." Redefine the ground so the competitors' traditional
advantages look clumsy, bloated, or outdated.

Questions this lens asks:
- What stage of sophistication is this market in?
- What is the Unique Mechanism that explains how you deliver the result?
- What is the universal compromise all other competitors force on the buyer?
- What apparent flaw or constraint can you flip into your greatest selling asset?
